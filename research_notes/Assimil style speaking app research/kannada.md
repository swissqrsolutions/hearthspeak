# Kannada (Bengaluru colloquial) — research notes for a 100+ lesson Assimil-style speaking course

Notation: simplified Latin with long vowels as ā ī ū ē ō and retroflexes as ṭ ḍ ṇ ḷ (ISO 15919-like). Items marked **[AK]** are from the researcher's background knowledge of spoken Bengaluru Kannada and were NOT confirmed by a source fetched in this session; a native-speaker reviewer should check them before use.

## 1. Spoken vs written Kannada (diglossia) and which variety to teach

### Takeaway
Kannada has a real gap between the formal written/literary form (*grānthika*) and the spoken forms, but the gap is smaller than in Tamil. The spoken standard is the Bengaluru–Mysuru (southern) variety of educated urban speakers. This is what films, radio and plays use, and it is the right target for a Bengaluru course. Teach colloquial forms from lesson 1, and bring in the formal forms later for reading and recognition.

### Cited Findings
- A diglossia, or split, exists between formal literary varieties and spoken varieties. — [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Kannada-language)
- Sources disagree on how wide the gap is. A Heidelberg Kannada manual says "there is no standard colloquial Kannada," that colloquial speech has regional and social peculiarities, and that differences between the written norm and most spoken dialects "are not great." Other sources say the spoken and written forms "sound completely different." — [Heidelberg manual (search snippet; the page itself was bot-blocked)](https://hasp.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/catalog/view/736/1242/90991)
- Urban southern Karnataka speakers shorten 1st-person and masculine endings: written *bandenu* is spoken *bande*, and *māḍidanu* is spoken *māḍida*. Conditionals drop a vowel: *māḍidare* becomes *māḍidre*. Some varieties contract *kareyuttēne* to *karētīni*. Colloquial forms are "increasingly used also in written Kannada," for example in fiction dialogue. — [Heidelberg manual (search snippet)](https://hasp.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/catalog/view/736/1242/90991)
- One grammar cites Schiffman (1983) for the view that the spoken standard ("Standard Kannada") is the variety of "educated urban middle class speakers in the area of Bangalore," and that colloquial forms come from formal forms by regular rules. — [search summary citing Schiffman](https://wals.info/refdb/record/Schiffman-1983)
- The standard form is based on the Mysore–Bangalore variety, which is "more or less consistent throughout Karnataka." Dialect groups are Southern, Northern, Central and Coastal, and most are mutually intelligible. — [Wikipedia: Kannada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannada)
- The Central Institute of Indian Languages (CIIL) published a book on "standard spoken Kannada, the dialect of Kannada spoken in Bangalore-Mysore area" (1995). — [search result listing (Penn/CIIL)](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/plc/kannada/)
- Spoken negation uses the periphrastic *illa*: *-uvudu + illa* (non-past), *-alu + illa* (past, e.g. *māḍalilla*), spoken present *māḍuttilla*, and the short *-alla* (*māḍalla*). The finite negative (*māḍenu*) is mainly literary. — [Wikipedia: Kannada grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannada_grammar)
- In speech, the 1st person singular and 3rd masculine singular lose the final *-nu*. Spoken past forms differ from literary ones: literary *kādu* is spoken *kāyidu*, and literary *nakku* is spoken *nakkidu*. The aorist-future in *-va* is limited to educated writing. The present-future in *-utta* covers both present and future. Gendered casual imperatives (*-ō* to males, *-ē* to females) are used with friends, children and animals. — [Wikipedia: Kannada grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannada_grammar)
- Colloquial present suffixes (written → spoken): 1sg *-ēne → -īni/-ini*; 1pl *-ēve → -īvi/-ivi*; 2pl *-īri → -īra*; neuter *-ade → -e* (e.g. *adu baratte*). Colloquial past: 1sg *-enu → -e*, 1pl *-evu → -vi* (*nāvu hōgidvi* "we went"), 3sg masc *-anu → -a*. Colloquial speech uses the present for the future with time words. *iru* "be" has the colloquial stem *irtā* (*alli irtāne* "he'll be there"). Negative question: *-lla → -lvā* (*nīnu nōḍalvā?*). — [sharmaeklavya2 Kannada notes: verbs](https://sharmaeklavya2.github.io/notes/kannada/grammar/04-verbs.html)
- **Regional varieties:**
  - **North Karnataka/Dharwad:** described as "bold and rustic." Final *-e* becomes *-i* (*mani* for *mane* "house"), with heavy Marathi borrowing. Despite being the "core" Kannada area, it carries low prestige among speakers from other regions.
  - **Mangaluru/coastal:** clear diction, close to how schools teach Kannada, with Tulu/Konkani/Beary influence.
  - **Mysuru/Bengaluru/Mandya:** seen as softer and more "refined."
  - The Karnataka Official Language Act 1963 names no variety as official.
  - Note: these are journalistic sources, not peer-reviewed. — [Star of Mysore](https://starofmysore.com/which-kannada-do-you-speak-mysore-dharwad-or-kundapra/); [Deccan Herald "One language, many voices" (search snippet; 403 on fetch)](https://www.deccanherald.com/amp/story/specials/one-language-many-voices-3779637); [Wikipedia: Mangaluru Kannada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mangaluru_Kannada)
- Bengaluru Kannada is "primarily spoken by youth and in informal discourse between locals." Bangalore and Mysore Kannada are the most common forms in plays and movies. Radio stations spread it among youth and the IT workforce. — [Wikipedia: Bengaluru Kannada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengaluru_Kannada)

### Inferences
- **Target variety:** educated Bengaluru–Mysuru colloquial, with Bengaluru urban lexis (Kanglish, slang) as an extra layer. It has the highest prestige and the most media exposure, and outsiders in Bengaluru hear it most. Add a single "dialect awareness" lesson at B2+ (Dharwad *-i* endings, *bārri/hōgri*, Mangaluru clarity) for listening only.
- **Core colloquial vs formal contrasts to teach explicitly [AK]:**
  - *māḍuttēne → māḍtīni* (I do/will do)
  - *māḍuttāne → māḍtāne*
  - *māḍuttade → māḍutte / māḍatte*
  - *ide* ("is/exists" for neuter, the same in both registers) vs *idāne/idāre*
  - *iddēne → idīni* (I am)
  - *māḍuttiddēne → māḍtā idīni → māḍtidīni* (I am doing)
  - *hōguttēne → hōgtīni*
  - *bandiddāne → bandidāne*
  - *ēnu → ēn*; *yāke*; *illa*; *alla*; *beku/bēḍa*; *gottu/gottilla*
  - *āgutte → āgatte*; *āytu* (done/OK)
  - Contractions: *nanage → nange*, *nimage → nimge*, *avanige → avnige*, *avanu → avnu*, *avaḷu → avḷu*, *avaru → avru*, *ivattu* (today), *nāḷe* (tomorrow), *swalpa* (a little), *beku-anta → bēkanta*.
- **Assimil dialogues:** write them in colloquial forms. Each lesson can carry a footnote giving the formal form, which works as "passive register training."

### Gaps
- I did not find a fetched, authoritative paradigm table of Bengaluru colloquial verb forms (Schiffman 1983 and the Heidelberg manual were inaccessible: bot-blocked or 403). The [AK] forms above need native review.
- I found no quantitative data on how far the written and spoken forms differ.

## 2. Grammar sequencing

### Takeaway
Kannada is SOV and agglutinative. It has about 7 morphological cases (nom, acc *-annu/-anna*, instr-abl *-inda*, dat *-(i)ge/-kke*, gen *-a/-ina*, loc *-alli*, voc). The verb agrees with the subject in person, number and (in the 3rd person) gender, and plural pronouns are used as honorifics. Dative-experiencer constructions (*nange … bēku/gottu/iṣṭa*) are both high-frequency and grammatically easy, so they belong very early, as in Penn's spoken-Kannada preliminary lessons.

### Cited Findings
- **Cases.** The suffixes attach to an oblique stem:
  - nominative *-u* (*tammanu*)
  - accusative *-annu/-anna* (*tammannu*)
  - instrumental-ablative *-inda* (*tammaninda*, *kāḍininda*)
  - dative *-(i)ge*, *-kke* for class III (*tammanige*, *marakke* "to the tree")
  - genitive *-a/-ina* (*tammana*, *kāḍina*)
  - locative *-alli* (*tammanalli*)
  - vocative *-ē* (*tammanē*)
  - Plurals take *-gaḷu*, *-(a)ru*. — [Wikipedia: Kannada grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannada_grammar)
- **Formal non-past endings:** 1sg *-ēne*, 1pl *-ēve*, 2sg *-(īy)e*, 2pl *-īri*, 3sg.m *-āne*, 3sg.f *-āḷe*, 3pl *-āre*, neuter *-ade*/*-ave*. Northern varieties instead use *-dahe*-type forms. Past marker *-(i)du*: *iru → iddu*, *hōgu → hōgi/hōyi/hōdu*. — [Wikipedia: Kannada grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannada_grammar)
- **Honorifics:**
  - *nīvu* (polite/plural "you") vs *nīnu* (intimate)
  - *avaru/ivaru* (honorific) vs *avanu/avaḷu*
  - Spoken Kannada can add *-gaḷu* (*nīvugaḷu*).
  - *ātanu/āke* can sound aloof or disrespectful.
  - *tāvu* is deferential but now theatrical or sarcastic.
  - Usage varies: some speakers address their mother with *nīnu*, others find that disrespectful. — [Wikipedia: Kannada grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannada_grammar)
- **Imperatives:**
  - Plain 2sg = the root (*māḍu*)
  - Polite *-i* (*māḍi*); plural/polite *-iri*
  - Hortative *-ōṇa* (*hōgōṇa* "let's go")
  - 3rd person *-ali* (*bartāne-ali*, i.e. "let him…")
  - Irregular *bā* (come), *tā* (bring); plural *banni*, *tanni* (Northern *barri*)
  - Casual *-ō/-ē* — [Wikipedia: Kannada grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannada_grammar)
- **Non-finite forms:**
  - Conditional *-are* on the past stem (*bandare*, *māḍidare*; spoken *bandre*, *māḍidre*)
  - Present adverbial *-utta*
  - Adjectival past *-a* (*bareda* "written") and non-past *-va*
  - Negative participles *-ada* (adjectival) and *-ade* (adverbial, "without doing")
  - Continuous = *-utta + iru*; perfect = past stem + *iru*
  - Prohibitive with *bēḍa* (*hōgbēḍa* "don't go")
  - Admonitive *bāradu/kūḍadu* — [Wikipedia: Kannada grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannada_grammar); [Heidelberg (snippet)](https://hasp.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/catalog/view/736/1242/90991)
- **Negation:** *-alla* present/habitual (*nōḍalla* "I don't/won't see"), *-alilla* past (*nōḍalilla*), *-uvudilla* (formal future), *iralla* "won't be". Tag question *-lvā* (*nōḍalvā?*). — [sharmaeklavya2 notes](https://sharmaeklavya2.github.io/notes/kannada/grammar/04-verbs.html)
- **Penn's "Spoken Kannada: Preliminary Lessons" sequence:**
  1. Yes–no questions
  2. Basic vocabulary; existence and location
  3. Interrogatives, dative case, verbs of motion
  4. "Dative-stative" constructions
  5. Liking/wanting (dative-stative)
  6. Asking about food — [Penn PLC Kannada](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/plc/kannada/)

### Inferences (proposed sequence for ~110 lessons; colloquial forms) [AK for example forms]
1. **L1–10 (A1):**
   - Equational sentences with no copula (*idu nīru* "this is water"), *alla* (*idu kāphi alla*)
   - Existence/location *ide/illa* (*alli ide*, *illa*)
   - Question *-ā* (*kāphi ide-ā?* → *kāphi idyā?*); WH-words *ēnu/ēn, yāru, yelli, yāke, yāvāga, hēge, eṣṭu*
   - Pronouns, and *nīvu* by default
   - Dative experiencer chunks: *nange … bēku / bēḍa / gottu / gottilla / iṣṭa / sāku*
   - Numbers and money (*eṣṭu?*), greetings (*namaskāra, hēgidīra? chennāgidīni, ūṭa āytā?*)
2. **L11–25:**
   - Non-past (*hōgtīni, bartīni, māḍtīni*) across all persons
   - Cases: dative *-ge* (destination: *ōphīsge hōgtīni*), locative *-alli*, accusative *-anna/-na*, genitive
   - Polite imperatives *banni, kūtkoḷḷi, swalpa nilsi*
   - Negative *-alla*
   - Clitics *-ū* (also/even) and *-ē* (emphasis: *nānē*)
   - Tag *alvā/alwa*
3. **L26–45 (A2):**
   - Past tense colloquial (*bande, hōde, māḍde, nōḍde*); past negative *-lilla*
   - Progressive *-tā idīni/-tidīni*
   - Instrumental-ablative *-inda/-ninda* (*Jayanagar-inda*)
   - Infinitive *-alu/-ok* + *bēku* (obligation: *nānu hōgbēku*); *āgatte/āgalla* (can/can't: *nange māḍōkāgalla*)
   - Gendered casual imperatives
   - Echo words (*kāphi-gīphi*)
4. **L46–65 (B1):**
   - Conditional *-dre* (*bandre*), concessive *-drū*
   - Converb chains (*ūṭa māḍi bande*)
   - Compound/vector verbs: *biḍu* (completive: *hōgbiṭṭa*), *koḷḷu* (reflexive/self-benefactive: *tagoḷḷi*), *hāku* (*bareduhāku*), *iḍu*, *nōḍu* (try)
   - Quotative *anta/ante* (hearsay "they say")
   - Doubt particle *-ō* (*bartāneō illvō*)
   - Perfect *-idīni*
5. **L66–85 (B2):**
   - Relative participles *-o/-ida* (*nānu nōḍida sinima*, *bartiro bus*); negative *-ada/-ade*
   - Verbal nouns
   - Causatives *-isu* (*māḍisu*, *kalisu*)
   - Permissive/hortative (*hōgōṇa, hōgli*)
   - Reported speech; *-ante* modal
   - Honorific shifts with elders
6. **L86–110 (C1–C2):**
   - Formal register (*-uttēne*, *-uvudilla*), news Kannada, Sanskrit-derived vocabulary (*tatsama*)
   - Proverbs (*gāde*)
   - Dialect listening (Dharwad/Mangaluru)
   - Rhetoric, argumentation, humour, film dialogue

## 3. High-frequency vocabulary and frequency resources

### Takeaway
I found no ready-made spoken Kannada frequency list. Usable corpora are Leipzig (Wikipedia-based), Sketch Engine knWaC (~11M words) and "Kannada Trends" (~45M), plus AI4Bharat IndicCorp (not verified here). All are written or web text, so spoken frequency has to be approximated, for example from film subtitles or code-mixed social media corpora.

### Cited Findings
- Sketch Engine: Kannada Trends corpus (~45M words) and Kannada Web 2012 (knWaC12, ~11M words). It has a word-list tool, but access is trial/paid. — [Sketch Engine](https://www.sketchengine.eu/corpora-and-languages/kannada-text-corpora/)
- Leipzig Corpora Collection hosts Kannada Wikipedia corpora (kan_wikipedia_2014_10K, 2018_100K) with frequency statistics. — [Leipzig CLS](https://cls.corpora.uni-leipzig.de/en/kan_wikipedia_2014_10K/5.1.18_Words%20nearly%20always%20as%20next%20neighbors.html)
- Wiktionary's frequency-lists page had no visible Kannada entry. — [Wiktionary](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Wiktionary%3AFrequency_lists)
- Code-mixed Kanglish datasets exist for building a "spoken-ish" list:
  - CoLI-Kanglish (ICON 2022): word-level language ID on Roman-script Kannada-English text — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2022.icon-wlli.8)
  - Kanglish NER corpus (WNUT 2022) — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2022.wnut-1.17)
  - ICON 2020 corpus — [ACL Anthology](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/2020.icon-main.13.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Core spoken function words and verbs to front-load [AK]:**
  - Existence and copula: *ide, illa, alla, idīni, iddāre*
  - Need/knowledge/liking: *bēku, bēḍa, gottu, gottilla, iṣṭa, sāku*
  - Common verbs: *āytu, āgatte, āgalla, hōgu, bā/banni, māḍu, koḍu, tago, nōḍu, hēḷu, kēḷu, tinnu, kuḍi, kūtko, nillu*
  - Adverbs and fillers: *swalpa, tumbā, jāsti, kammi, bēga, nidhāna, ivattu, nāḷe, ninne, īga, āmēle, munche*
  - Particles: *anta, alvā, -ū, -ē, -ā*
  - Discourse markers: *haudu/hū* (yes), *sari* (ok), *parvāgilla* (never mind/no problem), *biḍi*, *hōgli*
- **Frequency method:** build a spoken list from a Kannada OpenSubtitles or YouTube auto-caption corpus (not verified to exist at scale), cross-checked against the Leipzig lists.

## 4. Kanglish (code-mixing) and modern Bengaluru slang

### Takeaway
Bengaluru speech freely inserts English nouns and verbs. The English word takes Kannada case suffixes (*companyge*) or a light verb (*adjust maadi*, *enjoy maadi*). Slang such as *maga, guru, macha, sakkath, bombāṭ, bejān, chindi, kirik, sisya* is well attested. It is casual and in-group, so it is not for the workplace.

### Cited Findings
- **Kanglish patterns:** mixing happens at phrase, word, syntactic and morphological level, e.g. *companyge* (English noun + Kannada dative). — [ICON 2020](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/2020.icon-main.13.pdf); [WNUT 2022](https://aclanthology.org/2022.wnut-1.17)
- Kannada–English code-switching occurs both within and between sentences, and expresses traditional-modern and local-global identity (Shivaprasad 2015 MA thesis). — [IDEALS Illinois](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/79735)
- **Popular Kanglish phrases:** *"swalpa adjust maadi"*, *"just maja maadi"* ("chill out", spread by a radio station), *"Enjoy maadi"* (a radio station name). — [Wikipedia: Bengaluru Kannada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengaluru_Kannada)
- **Slang meanings (Wikipedia):**
  - *sakkath* — awesome/cool
  - *bejān* — a lot
  - *kuḍumi* — bookworm
  - *chamak / dose* — scare someone playfully
  - *sisya* — trusted buddy
  - *chindi* — amazing
  - *boni* — first sale of the day (from Telugu)
  - *KD* — cunning person
  - *tight āgbiṭṭidāne* — he's very drunk
  - *meter* — a measure of bravery
  - *thukāli* — useless person
  - *kirik āgtide* — a fight is happening — [Wikipedia: Bengaluru Kannada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengaluru_Kannada)
- *maga* is the most common "dude." *macha*, *guru* and *bro* are also used. *bombāṭ* = awesome (*"Ā cinema bombāṭ āgide!"*). *baddimaga* = "idiot," affectionate only among close friends. Avoid slang at work. — [Preply: Kannada slang](https://preply.com/en/blog/kannada-slang-words/); [Holidify](https://www.holidify.com/pages/slangs-of-bangalore-1716.html)
- Instagram accounts such as @chummatalks teach a daily conversational word or phrase. — [The South First](https://thesouthfirst.com/karnataka/bridging-the-language-divide-how-non-locals-in-bengaluru-are-learning-kannada/)

### Inferences [AK — needs native check for 2026 currency]
- **Other common items:**
  - *scene illa* (no chance/not happening), *full* (totally: *full tight*, *full busy*), *ultimate*
  - *jakkās* (excellent), *lakṣaṇa*, *dabba* (dumb), *loose* (crazy), *kachaḍa* (rubbish)
  - *sumne* (for no reason/just), *chill māḍi*, *bēga bā maga*, *ēn guru?*
  - *bombāṭ ūṭa*, *seen-u* (Kanglish *-u* added to English words), *sāku biḍu*
- **Kanglish morphology to teach explicitly:**
  - English verb + *māḍu/āgu*: *call māḍtīni*, *cancel āytu*, *late āytu*, *book māḍi*, *share māḍu*
  - English nouns + case: *officege, metro-alli, Uber-alli, laptop-anna*
  - The epenthetic *-u* on English words (*bus-u, scene-u, plan-u*)
- **Loanword layers:** Urdu/Persian (*parvāgilla, jāsti, kharchu, sāku*?), Tamil (*macha*), Telugu (*boni*), English (heavy). Only *boni* is sourced (Telugu).

## 5. Existing courses, books and apps (and gaps)

### Takeaway
The market is apps with phrasebook/flashcard depth, YouTube channels (Kannada Kali is the biggest), CIIL/Penn academic materials, and "Learn Kannada in 30 days"-style books. None offers a long, graded, dialogue-driven colloquial course reaching B2+ with natural Kanglish. That gap is exactly what an Assimil-style course could fill.

## 6. Script, transliteration and pronunciation

### Takeaway
Kannada script is a near-phonetic abugida with 49 base letters (13 vowels, 34 consonants, plus anusvara/visarga) and many conjuncts (*ottakṣara*). ISO 15919 is the standard romanisation. Pronunciation priorities are vowel length, the retroflex series (ṭ ḍ ṇ ḷ ṣ), and dental vs retroflex. Aspirates are rare in native words and often dropped in casual speech.

### Cited Findings
- **Script:** "almost entirely phonetic." 49 phonemic letters (13 vowels, 34 consonants, 2 marks: anusvara, visarga). Conjunct forms make the symbol inventory much larger. The ISO 15919 sample appears in the article. — [Wikipedia: Kannada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannada)
- **Vowels:** five qualities, short vs long (i/iː, u/uː, e/eː, o/oː, a/aː).
- **Retroflexes:** /ɳ ʈ ɖ ʂ ɭ/; /ʂ/ is often realised as /ʃ/.
- **Aspirates:** "very rarely occur in native vocabulary." They are mostly in loanwords, and many speakers drop aspiration in informal speech.
- **Colloquial glides:** initial *i/e* get a y- onset, initial *u/o* get a w- onset (*yeṣṭu*, *wondu* for *ondu*). Final *-e → -i* in northern dialects. — [Wikipedia: Kannada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannada)
- Bright (1970) specifically treats the phonological rules between literary and colloquial Kannada. — [cited in Wikipedia: Kannada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannada)
- Kanglish social-media text is overwhelmingly in Roman script, which is why the CoLI-Kanglish task targets "Roman script" words. — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2022.icon-wlli.8)

### Inferences
- **Recommendation:** teach in a learner-friendly Latin transcription (ISO 15919-lite: macrons for length, underdots for retroflexes) from day 1, because the course is about speaking.
  - Introduce Kannada script in parallel micro-lessons from about L10. Bengaluru signage, bus boards, menus and WhatsApp are bilingual, and the script is near-phonetic, so it is learnable in 3–4 weeks.
  - By B1, show the script first.
  - Also show the informal "WhatsApp Romanization" (e.g. *maadi, beku, illa, hogi*, no diacritics) so learners can read real chats.
- **Pronunciation drills:**
  - Length pairs (*kalu* "learn" / *kālu* "leg")
  - Dental/retroflex pairs (*t/ṭ*, *d/ḍ*, *n/ṇ*, *l/ḷ*)
  - Geminates (*akka, amma, idde*)
  - Word-final *-u* on English loans
  - Don't over-aspirate.

### Gaps
- I found no source on how learners actually prefer script vs transliteration in Kannada apps.

## 7. Daily-life scenarios graded A1 → C2

### Takeaway
The scenario list below is a design proposal. It draws on the Bengaluru contexts named in the brief and on the learner context reported by The South First (workplaces, daily life, the language-imposition debate). It is not a sourced curriculum.

### Cited Findings
- Penn's preliminary spoken lessons start with yes/no questions, existence/location, motion verbs, dative-stative (liking/wanting) and food. This supports putting darshini/food and directions very early. — [Penn PLC](https://ccat.sas.upenn.edu/plc/kannada/)
- Newcomers in Bengaluru face barriers in daily life and the workplace. Debate over "imposition" vs inclusion is live, which makes language politics a valid advanced (B2/C1) discussion topic. — [The South First](https://thesouthfirst.com/karnataka/bridging-the-language-divide-how-non-locals-in-bengaluru-are-learning-kannada/)
- *boni* (first sale of the day) and "swalpa adjust maadi" are attested market/transport phrases. — [Wikipedia: Bengaluru Kannada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bengaluru_Kannada)

### Inferences (proposed ladder; key structures in brackets) [AK for phrases]

**A1 (L1–20)**
1. Greetings and "*Ūṭa āytā?*" / "*Hēgidīra?*" (*ide/illa*, *nīvu*)
2. Introducing yourself and *Kannaḍa swalpa swalpa gottu* (*gottu/gottilla*)
3. Darshini breakfast: *ondu masāle dōse, eraḍu idli-vaḍe, by-two kāphi* (numbers, *bēku*)
4. Paying and prices: *eṣṭu āytu?*
5. Auto-rickshaw: *Indiranagar-ge bartīrā? mīṭar hāki* (dative, imperative)
6. Asking directions: *metro station yelli ide?* (locative)
7. Family words; "this/that"
8. Time and days: *yeṣṭu gaṇṭe?*
9. Vegetable market: *kilo eṣṭu? swalpa kammi māḍi*
10. Phone basics: *ēnu? kēḷstilla, āmēle call māḍtīni*

**A2 (L21–45)**
- BMTC bus / Namma Metro (tickets, stops, *iḷkoḷḷi*)
- Ordering food delivery and calling the delivery person
- Apartment/landlord: rent, advance, water problem
- Neighbours and the security guard
- Weather and rain/traffic small talk (*tumbā traffic!*)
- Shopping for clothes
- Telling what you did yesterday (past tense)
- Weekend plans (*-ōṇa*)
- WhatsApp messages and voice notes
- Haggling with an auto driver who refuses the meter (*-alla, bēḍa*)

**B1 (L46–70)**
- Office talk in a tech company (standup, *meeting-ge late āytu*, Kanglish)
- Doctor and pharmacy (dative experiencer: *nange jvara ide, talenōvu*)
- Bank / UPI problems
- Temple visit etiquette
- Visiting elders' homes (honorifics, *kūtkoḷḷi*, refusing more food politely)
- Festivals: Ugadi (*bēvu-bella*), Sankranti (*eḷḷu-bella*, *eḷḷu bīri*), Dasara in Mysuru, Deepavali
- Cricket/IPL and RCB fandom ("*Ee sala cup namde*")
- Kannada cinema and actors
- Complaints (to BBMP/GBA, courier, landlord), with conditionals
- Making excuses and apologising

**B2 (L71–90)**
- Wedding invitations and ceremonies
- Arguing about traffic and infrastructure
- Hearsay and gossip (*ante*, quotative)
- Relative clauses in storytelling
- Job interview in Kannada
- Kannada Rajyotsava (Nov 1) and language pride
- Renting/buying property negotiation
- Police/RTO
- Kids' schooling and parent meetings
- Humour and banter with friends (slang register)

**C1–C2 (L91–110+)**
- Politics and the language debate (Hindi imposition, signboard rules)
- News Kannada (formal register)
- Proverbs (*gādegaḷu*)

- Dialect listening: Dharwad, Mangaluru, Mysuru
- Film dialogue analysis
- Debates and persuasive speech
- Condolence/funeral language
- Formal speech at a function
- Telling long anecdotes
- Rapid Kanglish office negotiation
- Sarcasm and indirectness
