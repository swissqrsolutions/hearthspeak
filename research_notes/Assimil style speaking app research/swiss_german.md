# Swiss German (Schweizerdeutsch) for an Assimil-style speaking course (~100+ lessons, 2026 colloquial target)

> Method note: these notes combine web sources (cited inline) with the researcher's own knowledge of Zürich German. Anything **not** backed by a source is labelled "(researcher knowledge, verify with a native Zürich speaker)" and kept in **Inferences**. Some popular blogs give non-Zürich or garbled forms (e.g. "I ha gfunda", "ganga", "Ankche"); these are flagged where relevant. A Zürich native speaker should review every lesson script.

---

## 1. Which dialect to teach, and how to spell it

### Takeaway
Teach **Zürich German (Züritüütsch)**, written in a moderate, readable "WhatsApp-style" spelling based on Dieth principles: Zürich is the largest and most economically dominant dialect area, is central/eastern High Alemannic, and is easy to understand nationwide. Bernese is the best-liked dialect and a credible second track, but it diverges more (e.g. l-vocalization, a two-form verb plural). There is no official orthography. Choose one consistent, learner-friendly convention and show common chat variants alongside it.

### Cited Findings
- "Swiss German" is an umbrella term for Alemannic dialects, not one variety. There are about 4.93 million native speakers (2013), and about 87% of German-speaking Swiss used Swiss German in daily life in 2014. — [Wikipedia: Swiss German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_German)
- Dialect groups are Low Alemannic (Basel, Lake Constance), High Alemannic (most of the Plateau, split west/middle/east, with Zürich in the middle group) and Highest Alemannic (Valais/Walser). — [Wikipedia: Swiss German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_German)
- **Medial diglossia:** dialect is spoken by all social classes with no stigma, while Standard German is used for writing, school, main news, parliament and with non-Alemannic speakers. — [Wikipedia: Swiss German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_German)
- Zürich German has six traditional sub-varieties (city, Lake Zürich, Winterthur and others), and they are becoming more homogeneous. — [Wikipedia: Zurich German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zurich_German)
- Zürich German is "the dialect of Switzerland's largest city and canton" and dominates the region. Basel German resembles Alsatian, and Bernese has French-influenced upper-class and Matte-quarter variants. — [Preply blog](https://preply.com/en/blog/swiss-german-vs-german)
- **Bernese is the most popular dialect** in a Blick reader poll, and SRF ran a feature titled "Beliebtester Dialekt: Bärenstarkes Berndeutsch – warum eigentlich?" (methodology: a reader poll, not representative). — [Blick](https://www.blick.ch/community/auaeae-scho-berndeutsch-ist-der-beliebteste-dialekt-der-blick-leser-id15336476.html); [SRF](https://www.srf.ch/radio-srf-1/mundart/beliebtester-dialekt-baerenstarkes-berndeutsch-warum-eigentlich)
- Eastern dialects such as Zürich German have **one plural verb ending for all three persons** (mir/ihr/si mal-ed). Western dialects such as Bernese distinguish two endings. — [de.Wikipedia: Alemannische Grammatik](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemannische_Grammatik)
- In Bernese, /l/ is often realized as [w] (l-vocalization). — [Wikipedia: Swiss German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_German)
- Dialects are converging toward regional "Grossagglomerationsdialekte" because of migration and mobility. — [de.Wikipedia: Schweizerdeutsch](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schweizerdeutsch)
- Learner-oriented advice is to pick one dialect (Züritüütsch or Bärndütsch) and use matching materials. — [ausbildung-weiterbildung.ch FAQ](https://www.ausbildung-weiterbildung.ch/en-US/Bildungshilfe/Faq/Schwizerduetsch-lernen-Gibt-es-tatsaechlich-auch-Lehrmittel-auf-Schweizerdeutsch); [Betranslated 2026 guide](https://www.betranslated.com/blog/learning-swiss-german)
- **Orthography:**
  - There are no official rules. Writers either stay close to Standard German spelling or write phonetically. — [Wikipedia: Swiss German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_German)
  - Eugen Dieth's *Schwyzertütschi Dialäktschrift* (1938) was revised in 1986 as the typewriter-friendly *Dieth-Schreibung*. Its conventions include k/ck for the affricate [kx], gg for unaspirated fortis [k], y for long [iː] where Standard German has /aɪ/, ä mainly for [æ], ie only for the diphthong [ɪə], and w for [ʋ]. — [Wikipedia: Swiss German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_German); [de.Wikipedia: Schweizerdeutsch](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schweizerdeutsch)
  - Dieth's system "is rarely followed" for Zürich German. — [Wikipedia: Zurich German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zurich_German)
  - Sample spelling of a Zürich text: "Emaal händ de Biiswind und d Sune gschtritte…" (doubled vowels for length, *scht*). — [Wikipedia: Zurich German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zurich_German)
- **Chat spelling:**
  - Chatters write dialect inconsistently. Standard German spellings often win even in Zürich chat channels, and dialect schwa is often written "ä" as a dialect marker. — [Siebenhaar, IRC study (core.ac.uk)](https://core.ac.uk/download/14514123.pdf); [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/14514125.pdf)
  - In about 750,000 WhatsApp messages from about 600 chats ("What's up, Switzerland?"), chat partners accommodate each other's spellings within weeks, for example switching from "ned" to "nöd" ("not"). — [Horizons (SNSF magazine)](https://horizons-mag.ch/?p=6352); [Felder 2020, Benjamins](https://benjamins.com/catalog/scl.98.04fel)
  - The UZH WhatsApp corpus contains a nöd vs. ne(e)d comparison chart. — [Stark/Dürscheid, UZH](https://uzh.ch/cmsssl/spur/dam/jcr:b3ede26a-9bfb-414f-922c-74612bd4c145/31Stark_WhatsApp.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Recommendation: Zürich German as the main track.** It has the largest speaker base, a business/media/tech hub where expat learners concentrate, a uniform plural (simpler morphology) and nöd/öppis/chasch forms widely heard on Zürich-based media. Bernese could be an optional "dialect spotlight" every ~10 lessons (Grüessech, nid, i bi gsy, Bärn, -u for -l: "Mieu"/"Miuch"), plus Basel (Baseldytsch: "Grüezi" → "Grüessech/Sali", "nit", "y" vowels).
- **Recommended course spelling ("moderate Zürich chat orthography"):**
  - Double vowels for long vowels (Ziit, Huus, Lüüt, gaa, haa).
  - *ä* for open e/schwa-like endings where natives write it (mached / machäd: pick one; most natives write *-ed/-e*).
  - *sch* for /ʃ/ before t/p (Fescht, gschpürt).
  - *ch* everywhere for /x/ (Chind, Chuchi).
  - *ie/ue/üe* as diphthongs.
  - *ei* as written by locals (Zeit = Ziit, but "zwei", "Bei").
  - *y* only in names/historic spellings.
  - Avoid Dieth diacritics. Show 1–2 common variants in a sidebar, e.g. "ich/i", "nöd/nid/ned", "isch", "öppis/öpis", "chasch/kasch", "gsi/gsii", "hesch/häsch", "mir/mer". This mirrors real WhatsApp writing. (Researcher knowledge of typical chat practice, partially supported by the Siebenhaar and Felder findings.)
- Teach learners that natives *read and write Standard German* for most formal writing, so Swiss German text is mainly chats, social media, ads and songs. Audio is therefore primary, and the Assimil format should lean even more on audio than usual.

### Gaps
- There is no representative 2020s survey of which dialect is "most useful" for learners. The popularity data is reader polls only.
- I found no quantitative frequency table of chat spelling variants. The Felder and Stark PDFs were binary and could not be text-extracted in this session.
- "Nöis Gschmöis" (UZH dialect-change app project, launched October 2025) has no published results yet. — [SRF](https://www.srf.ch/news/schweiz/dialektforschung-mal-anders-noeis-gschmoeis-uni-zuerich-erforscht-das-neue-schweizerdeutsch)

## 2. Core grammar differences from Standard German, and lesson sequencing

### Takeaway
Swiss German grammar is simpler than Standard German in tense and case:
- **Tense:** no preterite (perfect only), no werden-future.
- **Case:** no genitive, and nominative = accusative for nouns/articles.

But it has its own features:
- the relative particle *wo* (with resumptive pronouns)
- obligatory verb doubling with *gaa/choo* (and optionally *laa/aafaa*)
- short irregular verbs (sii, haa, gaa, choo, tue, laa)
- clitic pronouns and linking -n-
- Konjunktiv II forms
- *am … sii* progressive
- dative constructions for possession
- a different verb-cluster order

All of these can be sequenced from lesson 1.

### Cited Findings
- **Tenses:**
  - There is no preterite indicative, and the perfect replaces it. A preterite subjunctive (Konjunktiv II) survives. The pluperfect is a "double perfect". — [Wikipedia: Swiss German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_German)
  - Double perfect example: "i ha's gmacht gha" ("I had done it"). — [de.Wikipedia: Alemannische Grammatik](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemannische_Grammatik)
  - Verbs that take *sein* keep it: "ich bin gegangen" → "I bi gange" (the source gives the non-Zürich form "ganga"). — [The Local](https://thelocal.ch/20180919/seven-ways-swiss-german-is-different-from-standard-german)
  - There is no werden-future. The present is used, often with a particle. — [de.Wikipedia: Alemannische Grammatik](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemannische_Grammatik)
  - Only present and perfect are used in practice. — [Preply](https://preply.com/en/blog/swiss-german-vs-german)
- **Genitive:**
  - There is no genitive case. The replacements are *von* (vom) phrases or dative + possessive pronoun: "em Adrian sy Hund". — [Wikipedia: Swiss German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_German); [de.Wikipedia: Alemannische Grammatik](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemannische_Grammatik)
  - "Em Vater sin Hund" / "de Hund vom Vater". — [The Local](https://thelocal.ch/20180919/seven-ways-swiss-german-is-different-from-standard-german)
- **Relative clauses:** all relative clauses use *wo*. When *wo* is not the subject or direct object, a resumptive pronoun appears: "e Maa, wo si nem zueluegt". — [Wikipedia: Swiss German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_German); [de.Wikipedia: Alemannische Grammatik](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemannische_Grammatik). The academic reference is Salzmann 2006, ch. 4 "Resumptives in Zurich German relative clauses". — [Wikipedia: Zurich German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zurich_German)
- **Verb doubling:**
  - *gaa* "go" and *choo* "come" double most strictly. *laa* "let" is less strict and optional in the past tense and in subordinate clauses. *aafaa* "start" is weakest and ungrammatical when doubled in the past or in subordinate clauses. — [Wikipedia: Swiss German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_German)
  - Example: "i gange ga schaffe" ("I'm going to work"). — [de.Wikipedia: Alemannische Grammatik](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemannische_Grammatik)
  - Variation in the field: "er laat de Schriiner cho" vs. "er laat la de Schriiner cho". — [SRF on Nöis Gschmöis](https://www.srf.ch/news/schweiz/dialektforschung-mal-anders-noeis-gschmoeis-uni-zuerich-erforscht-das-neue-schweizerdeutsch)
- **Verb cluster order:** clusters vary and can be cross-serial, the classic evidence that Swiss German is non-context-free. Example of the western order: "är het wölle cho". — [Wikipedia: Swiss German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_German); [de.Wikipedia: Alemannische Grammatik](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemannische_Grammatik)
- **Plural verbs:** Zürich has one plural ending for all three persons (mir/ihr/si mal-ed). — [de.Wikipedia: Alemannische Grammatik](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemannische_Grammatik)
- **Articles:**
  - Feminine singular and plural definite articles have two forms, depending on whether an adjective follows: "d Frou" vs. "di schöni Frou".
  - The indefinite article differs from the numeral: "e Maa" vs. "ei Maa".
  - Articles are used even with brand names: "I gang zum Aldi".
  - — [de.Wikipedia: Alemannische Grammatik](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemannische_Grammatik)
- **Pronouns:**
  - There are stressed and unstressed (clitic) forms: "mir singe" vs. "mer singe". — [de.Wikipedia: Alemannische Grammatik](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemannische_Grammatik)
  - Standard "wir" → "mir", and "sie" → "si". — [Preply](https://preply.com/en/blog/swiss-german-vs-german). Preply also says "ihr" → "dir", which is Bernese, not Zürich.
- **Linking -n-:** final -n is usually dropped (apocope) but reappears before a vowel: "I ha-n es Buech". — [de.Wikipedia: Schweizerdeutsch](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schweizerdeutsch)
- **Konjunktiv II:** forms such as "i malti, du maltisch, mir malted". Indicative "i mache" contrasts with subjunctive "i machi". — [de.Wikipedia: Alemannische Grammatik](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemannische_Grammatik); [de.Wikipedia: Schweizerdeutsch](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schweizerdeutsch)
- **zu-infinitive:** "z" + infinitive. Eastern dialects have -id forms (z ässid) and short verbs take -nd (z tond). — [de.Wikipedia: Alemannische Grammatik](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemannische_Grammatik)
- **Dative marker:** some dialects introduce the dative with *a/i*: "i gibe's a/i mynere Frou". — [de.Wikipedia: Alemannische Grammatik](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alemannische_Grammatik)
- **Word formation:**
  - The diminutive -li replaces -chen: Hüsli. — [The Local](https://thelocal.ch/20180919/seven-ways-swiss-german-is-different-from-standard-german)
  - Verbs in -ele (käfele "have a coffee"), agent nouns in -i (Laferi "chatterbox"), process nouns in -ete (Züglete "moving house").
  - -eln/-ern verbs become -(e)le/-(e)re (zügle "move house").
  - — [de.Wikipedia: Schweizerdeutsch](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schweizerdeutsch)
- **Numerals:** older speakers inflect "two" for gender (zwee Manne, zwo Fraue, zwäi Chind). — [de.Wikipedia: Schweizerdeutsch](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schweizerdeutsch)
- **Ongoing change:** the plural of Mann is shifting from Manne to Männer (unconfirmed), and Standard-German-like variants are spreading. Sentence structure changes more slowly than vocabulary. — [SRF](https://www.srf.ch/news/schweiz/dialektforschung-mal-anders-noeis-gschmoeis-uni-zuerich-erforscht-das-neue-schweizerdeutsch)

Regular verbs: mache – ich mache, du machsch, er macht, mir/ihr/si mached; participle gmacht.

**Articles:**
- Definite: de (m), d (f; plural), s (n). Before an adjective: di schöni Frau.
- Indefinite: en (m), e (f), es (n).
- Dative: em (m/n), de(r) (f), de (pl).
- Nominative = accusative.

**Pronouns:**
- Subject: ich/i, du (clitic -d/-sch), er (clitic -er/-en), si, es/s; mir/mer, ihr/er, si; formal Sie.
- Dative: mir/mer, dir/der, im/em, ire/ere; öis, öi, ine/ene.
- Possessive: mi(n), di(n), si(n), ire, öise, öie, ire.

**Konjunktiv II (very high frequency in polite speech):**
- wär, hett, chönnt, wett (would like), sött (should), müesst, dörft, würd.
- Analytic: "ich würd/tät" + infinitive.
- Synthetic: chiem (would come), gieng (would go), gäb (would give), nähm.
- The key A1 politeness phrase is "Ich hett gern…" (I'd like…).

**Other high-value constructions:**
- **Progressive:** "Ich bi am Ässe" ("I'm eating").
- **Doubling:** "Ich gang go poschte" ("I'm going shopping"), "Chunsch cho ässe?" ("Are you coming to eat?"), "Lömer's la sii" ("Let's leave it"), "Er fangt aa schaffe".
- **Future:** "Ich mach's dänn morn" ("I'll do it tomorrow then").
- **Subordinate clauses:** Zürich commonly has the order "dass er hät müese schaffe" (finite aux before the modal + infinitive) and "wil i nöd ha chöne cho" ("because I couldn't come").
- **Linking n:** "wie-n-i", "wo-n-er".
- **Negation:** nöd (Zürich) vs. nid (Bern/Basel) vs. ned (Aargau, Lucerne, etc.).
- **Modal particles:** gäll, halt, doch, scho, äbe, öppe.
- **Tag questions:** "…, oder?" and "…, gäll?".
- **"tue" periphrasis:** used in some dialects/childish register, "Er tuet ässe". Not core in adult Zürich speech, so mention it only as an aside.

**Suggested grammar sequencing across ~110 lessons:**
1. **L1–10:** sii/haa present, articles de/d/s, pronouns incl. mir/si, nöd, numbers 1–20, Grüezi/Hoi register (Sie vs du), "Ich hett gern…", questions (Wo? Wie? Was? Wänn? Wieviel?), "oder?".
2. **L11–20:** regular present, gaa/choo/wele/chöne/müese, gaa-doubling ("gang go poschte"), times and days (Mäntig…Sunntig), "am … sii" progressive, diminutive -li.
3. **L21–35:** perfect with haa/sii (gsi, gha, gange, cho, gmacht), irregular participles, dative articles/pronouns with prepositions (mit em Zug, bi de Migros), possessives, wo-relatives (subject/object).
4. **L36–50:** genitive replacement (em Peter sis Auto), Konjunktiv II politeness (wär, hett, chönnt, sött, würd), subordinate clauses (wil, dass, wänn, öb) and cluster order, comparatives (grösser als / wie), zu-infinitive (z ässe).
5. **L51–70:** laa/aafaa doubling, separable verbs, imperative (Chumm!, Lueg!, Gömmer!), resumptive wo-relatives (de Typ, wo-n-i mit em gredt ha), double perfect, clitic contractions (hätt-er-s-em gseit?), reported speech using Konj. II/I (er hät gseit, er heig/hett…).
6. **L71–90:** particles and pragmatics (halt, äbe, scho, gäll, öppe), irony/understatement, hedging (es chönnt sii, dass…), code-switching with English/Standard German.
7. **L91–110+:** dialect comparison (Bern/Basel/St. Gallen/Valais recognition), register shifts (Hochdeutsch in formal contexts), idioms, storytelling, debate.

## 3. High-frequency vocabulary, frequency sources and essential Helvetisms

### Takeaway
There is **no published, ready-made frequency list** for Swiss German. Corpora exist from which one can be derived: SwissCrawl, NOAH, the Swiss SMS Corpus, the "What's up, Switzerland?" WhatsApp corpus and Alemannic Wikipedia. A practical approach is to take a Standard German frequency core, map it to Zürich forms, and overlay a curated Helvetism and daily-life list.

### Cited Findings
- **Swiss SMS Corpus:** 25,947 SMS (~650k tokens) from 2009–2010, of which 41% are Swiss German dialect. — [sms.linguistik.uzh.ch](https://sms.linguistik.uzh.ch/)
- **"What's up, Switzerland?" WhatsApp corpus:** 617 chats, 763,650 messages, about 5.54 million tokens. — [whatsup.linguistik.uzh.ch](https://whatsup.linguistik.uzh.ch/_export/raw/01_corpus/start); [Linguistik Online article](https://bop.unibe.ch/linguistik-online/article/download/3849/5833?inline=1)
- **SwissCrawl:** web-crawled, described by its authors as the largest Swiss German text corpus. — [Linder et al., arXiv 1912.00159](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1912.00159)
- **NOAH's Corpus:** POS-tagged Swiss German texts of various genres, about 73k tokens per a 2025 SwissText listing. — [SwissText 2025](https://aclanthology.org/2025.swisstext-1.14.pdf)
- Swiss German "has been hardly used in written form" and has no standardized spelling, which complicates frequency counting. — [LREC 2018 paper](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/L18-1372.pdf)
- Alemannic Wikipedia has been called "about the most extensive resource for indirect learning". — [FluentU](https://www.fluentu.com/blog/german/learn-swiss-german/)
- **Helvetisms and dialect words with sources:**
  - Grüezi, En guete, morn (tomorrow), Friitig/Samschtig/Sunntig, Ziit, Gömmer? (shall we go?), Zmorge/Zmittag/Znacht (Preply gives "Zaabig", which is rarer), Weggli, Chueche, Schoggi, Kafi, chaufe, schaffe, Chasch…?, Billet, Velo, wider, kei, Ahnig, Bünzli, Chrüsimüsi, Röstigraben, Znüni, "de Fisch mache" (slip away). — [Preply](https://preply.com/en/blog/swiss-german-vs-german). Caution: Preply's "Ankche" should be Anke, and "Semmeli" is not Zürich.
  - French loans: Glace, merci, Velo, Trottoir. Swiss meanings: laufe = walk, springe = run. — [The Local](https://thelocal.ch/20180919/seven-ways-swiss-german-is-different-from-standard-german)
  - merssi, äxgüsi (excuse me), sali (hi, from French salut). English loans: tschuute (play football), sori, schoppe. — [de.Wikipedia: Schweizerdeutsch](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schweizerdeutsch)
  - Phrasebook items: Hoi, Grüezi/Grüessech, Guete Morge, Guete Abig, En Guete, Proscht, "Wieviel choschtet…?", "Wo häts…?", "Wo isch de nächscht Bankomat?", "Chan i mit Kreditkarte zahle?", "Was chönd Sie mir empfehle?", Gipfeli, Röschti, Chääs. Numbers: eis, zwei, drü, vier, foif, sächs, sibe, acht, nün, zäh, zwänzg, hundert. — [Wikivoyage Swiss German phrasebook](https://en.wikivoyage.org/wiki/Swiss_German_phrasebook)
  - Uf Wiederluege (goodbye), Mundart (dialect). — [Betranslated](https://www.betranslated.com/blog/learning-swiss-german)

## 4. Modern 2026 youth/urban slang, code-switching and multicultural Zürich

### Takeaway
2026 youth Swiss German is heavily English-infused ("peak", "deep", "easy", "nice", "chill") and shaped by a Zürich multiethnolect (Balkan/Turkish/Arabic influence: "wallah", voiced stops). The first official **Swiss Youth Word of the Year (2026, ZHAW)** gave **"Würd mi"** (#1), **"Nöd so deep"** (#2) and **"Peak"** (#3) for German-speaking Switzerland.

### Cited Findings
- **Swiss Youth Word of the Year 2026** (first edition, announced by ZHAW, chosen by young people nationwide):
  - German-speaking Switzerland: #1 "Würd mi" (short for "Das würd mi (ja) nerve", "that would annoy me"); #2 "Nöd so deep" ("don't take it so seriously / don't overthink"); #3 "Peak" (something especially good or successful).
  - Other language regions: Romandie "Goumin" (heartbreak), Ticino "Aura" (charisma), Romansh "Bursa" (wallet, ironic for a fool).
  - — [blue News](https://www.bluewin.ch/en/news/wurd-mi-is-the-first-german-swiss-youth-word-of-the-year-li.3650144); [ZHAW](https://www.zhaw.ch/de/ueber-uns/aktuell/news/detailansicht-news/event-news/jugendwort-des-jahres-schweiz-2026)
- **ZHAW Swiss-German Word of the Year 2025** (general, not youth): "Frauen-Nati", then "Zollhammer", then "Chlorhuhn". — [blue News search summary](https://www.bluewin.ch/en/news/womens-national-team-is-the-word-of-the-year-li.2990967)
- Germany's Jugendwort 2025 "Das crazy" is German, not Swiss, but German-media slang spreads into Swiss youth speech. — [blue News](https://www.bluewin.ch/en/news/das-crazy-is-the-youth-word-of-the-year-li.2922138)
- English mixing among young speakers: "dä Tescht isch easy gsi" instead of "eifach gsi". — [de.Wikipedia: Schweizerdeutsch](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schweizerdeutsch)
- **Multiethnolects:**
  - Multiethnolects have been observed among Swiss German youth since about 2000 but are not yet systematically described. — [Schmid/Morand/Schwab, LiLi](https://lipp.ub.uni-muenchen.de/lipp/article/download/4895/2770/7856)
  - Multilingual Zürich speakers use voiced lenis plosives as a sociophonetic marker. — [Morand et al., ICPhS 2019](https://assta.org/proceedings/ICPhS2019Microsite/pdf/full-paper_707.pdf)
  - Listeners perceive a continuum rather than a binary category. — [Loquens](https://loquens.revistas.csic.es/index.php/loquens/article/download/82/241?inline=1)
  - Media parody ethnolects ("secondary" ethnolects). — [Schmid 2017, VALS-ASLA](https://libra.unine.ch/bitstreams/a412e136-cefb-4f83-b0de-300302da9de2/download)
  - "Wallah" is documented as a generic intensifier in comparable urban youth varieties. Lexical studies for Switzerland are lacking. — [same search, Schmid et al.](https://lipp.ub.uni-muenchen.de/lipp/article/download/4895/2770/7856)
- A Zürich linguist says language change "always comes from below" and tends toward simplification. — [blue News / Blick](https://www.blick.ch/life/wissen/podcast/sprach-expertin-fordert-lasst-die-jugend-so-reden-wie-sie-reden-id21355851.html)

### Inferences
**Slang/colloquial register to teach**, tagged by age group and register (researcher knowledge, verify with Zürich speakers under 30):
- **Universal colloquial (all ages):**
  - Tags and fillers: oder?, gäll?, isch guet, passt, tipptopp, kei Stress.
  - Reactions: Hammer!, Läck!, Läck mir! (surprise), Gopfertelli / Gopferdammi (mild-to-strong curse), Seich (nonsense, "so en Seich"), Chabis (rubbish), Gugus (nonsense).
  - Phrases: es isch mer gliich / egal, kei Bock (ha), mega, huere (very; vulgar-tinged but ubiquitous among young people), geil (great), lässig, cool, nice.
  - Nouns: Stutz (franc), Bünzli (petty conformist), Grüsel (gross person).
  - Greetings: "Tschau zäme" / "Ciao zäme" (bye all), "Hoi zäme".
- **Youth/urban (2020s):** Würd mi, nöd so deep, peak, cringe, lost, sus, flexe, chille, easy, safe, real, wallah, Bro/Brudi, Digga (German import), "isch so", "ich schwör", "gönn dir", lit, "es git nüt Bessers". Many are pan-German or English internet slang adapted with Swiss morphology ("ich bi grad so lost gsi").
- **Code-switching:** English nouns and adjectives inserted with Swiss articles and participles ("Mir händ es Meeting", "Ich ha's gecancelt/gcancelt", "Das isch mega random", "Lass mal chillä"). French loans are part of the standard core (merci, Velo, Trottoir, Coiffeur, Glace, Billet, Portemonnaie). Italian loans: ciao, tschau, Pasta.
- **Course design:**
  - Teach the slang **passively first** (recognition), with register warnings (huere, gopferdammi).
  - Add an "update layer" since slang ages fast. Treat Jugendwort lists as yearly refresh content.
  - Learners using youth slang may sound odd. Recommend active use only of the "universal colloquial" tier.

### Gaps
- There is no systematic, sourced lexicon of current Zürich youth slang. Academic lexical studies of the Swiss multiethnolect are explicitly lacking.
- The ZHAW press-release PDF (full shortlist) could not be text-extracted.
- Specific claims about how common "huere", "Bro" or "wallah" are among Zürich youth in 2026 are unverified.

## 7. Pronunciation challenges

### Takeaway
The key hurdles are:
- uvular/velar **ch [x/χ] everywhere**, including word-initially (Chind, Chuchi)
- the **affricate k [kx]**
- **length-based fortis/lenis contrasts** with no voicing
- **consonant and vowel length as independent contrasts** (geminates)
- preserved **ie/ue/üe falling diphthongs**
- **monophthongs** where Standard German has diphthongs (Huus, Ziit, Lüüt)
- **open ä [æ]**
- scht/schp
- n-apocope with linking -n-
- loanword stress on the first syllable

### Cited Findings
- **Consonants:**
  - No voiced obstruents. The fortis/lenis contrast is preserved even word-finally (graad vs. Graat), unlike Standard German final devoicing. — [Wikipedia: Swiss German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_German)
  - In Zürich the lenis/fortis difference is **length** rather than voicing, with fortis obstruents longer. /x/ can be velar or uvular. — [Wikipedia: Zurich German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zurich_German)
  - /x/ is [x] (never [ç]). Aspirated stops occur mainly in loans and prefixed forms. — [Wikipedia: Swiss German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_German)
  - Dieth: k/ck = affricate [kx]; gg = unaspirated fortis [k]. — [Wikipedia: Swiss German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_German)
  - /r/ is traditionally an alveolar tap in Zürich, but uvular variants are now more frequent. There is no r-vocalization. — [Wikipedia: Zurich German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zurich_German)
  - Final -n is dropped in most dialects. — [Wikipedia: Swiss German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_German). Linking n before a vowel: "I ha-n es Buech". — [de.Wikipedia: Schweizerdeutsch](https://de.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schweizerdeutsch)
  - Bernese /l/ → [w]. — [Wikipedia: Swiss German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_German)
- **Length:** consonant length and vowel length vary independently, which is unusual among Germanic languages. — [Wikipedia: Swiss German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_German)
- **Vowels and diphthongs:**
  - The MHG diphthongs /iə, uə, yə/ are preserved (lieb, guet, Füess). Rounded front vowels are kept. — [Wikipedia: Swiss German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_German)
  - Standard German diphthongs correspond to long monophthongs (Haus → Huus, Zeit → Ziit). — [Betranslated](https://www.betranslated.com/blog/learning-swiss-german); [The Local](https://thelocal.ch/20180919/seven-ways-swiss-german-is-different-from-standard-german)
  - All Zürich diphthongs are falling, and /ə/ occurs only in unstressed syllables. — [Wikipedia: Zurich German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zurich_German)
  - Zürich /ai, au/ come from MHG /ei, ou/, while /ei, ou/ come from MHG /iː, uː/ in certain contexts. — [Wikipedia: Swiss German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_German)
  - Zürich traditionally had near-close vowels /ɪ ʏ ʊ/ as phonemes, but the distinction is being lost. /ɒ/ is often unrounded to [ɑ]. — [Wikipedia: Swiss German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swiss_German)
- **Sociophonetics:** voiced plosives occur as a multiethnolect marker among multilingual Zürich youth. — [Morand et al., ICPhS 2019](https://assta.org/proceedings/ICPhS2019Microsite/pdf/full-paper_707.pdf)
- **Prosody:** Swiss German rhythm is often perceived as slower and more melodic than northern German. — [Betranslated](https://www.betranslated.com/blog/learning-swiss-german)

### Inferences
**Pronunciation drill progression** (researcher design):
1. ch in all positions (Chuchichäschtli, Chind, acht, Chäs)
2. long vs. short vowels (Ziit/zit), monophthongs (Huus, Lüüt, Ziit)
3. ie/ue/üe diphthongs (lieb, guet, müed, Füess, Chüe)
4. open ä vs. e (Bär/Beeri)
5. scht/schp (Fescht, Wurscht, Chäschte)
6. fortis/lenis as length (Bode vs. Botte, lige vs. ligge), no final devoicing
7. affricate k [kx] (Kafi vs. Gaffi) and unaspirated p/t/k
8. n-linking (wie-n-er, wo-n-i)
9. first-syllable stress on French loans (Büro, Billet, Velo, Trottoir are stressed on the first syllable)
10. tap vs. uvular r (both acceptable)
11. Zürich intonation patterns and the "oder?" tag rise

**Avoid** teaching Standard German [ç] in "ich", which is the most salient foreign marker.
