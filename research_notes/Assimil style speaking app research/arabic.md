## 1. Diglossia and dialect choice (MSA vs Egyptian vs Levantine vs Gulf vs Darija), Assimil's offerings, and the MSA bridge

### Takeaway
No one acquires MSA natively, so a course that targets native-like *speaking* has to teach a dialect. The best primary target is **urban Levantine (Shami: a Damascus/Beirut/Amman-Jerusalem "common Levantine" koine)**, with **Egyptian as a planned receptive add-on** and a thin **MSA "bridge" strand** for reading and news. Egyptian is the strongest alternative: it has the most speakers and the widest media reach. Darija is the most useful dialect locally in French-speaking Switzerland, but it is the least portable and the hardest for other Arabs to understand. Assimil today sells only an MSA "sans peine" course (77 lessons) plus small dialect phrasebooks. A 100+ lesson course in dialect would fill a real gap.

### Cited Findings
**Diglossia and speakers**
- Colloquial Arabic has about 411 million native speakers (2020–2024 estimate). Varieties differ considerably by region. Mutual intelligibility often tracks geographic distance, and some far-apart varieties are mutually unintelligible — [Wikipedia: Varieties of Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varieties_of_Arabic)
- Intelligibility between Mashriqi (east) and Maghrebi (west) dialects is asymmetric. Maghrebi speakers understand Mashriqi better than the reverse, and that ability comes "mostly due to the widespread popularity of Egyptian and Levantine popular media (for example Syrian or Lebanese TV shows)." Egyptians report difficulty understanding North Africans — [Wikipedia: Varieties of Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varieties_of_Arabic)
- Egyptian is "one of the most widely understood Arabic dialects" because of 20th-century Egyptian radio, TV and film — [Wikipedia: Varieties of Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varieties_of_Arabic)
- Speaker counts conflict across secondary sources:
  - Egyptian: 68M (an older Ethnologue figure) to "close to 100 million". Qasid cites about 115M native speakers in Egypt.
  - Levantine: about 37–38M (North Levantine about 25M plus South Levantine about 12M).
  - Gulf: about 7–10M.
  - Moroccan and Algerian: about 33M each (grouped as Maghrebi).
  - Sources: [Lingoda](https://www.lingoda.com/blog/en/how-many-people-speak-arabic-around-the-world/); [Middle East Eye](https://www.middleeasteye.net/discover/five-major-spoken-arabic-dialects-unique); [Qasid](https://www.qasidonline.com/blog/the-ultimate-guide-to-arabic-dialects). Note: the Levantine and Gulf figures look low. Ethnologue's counts are by edition and by ISO code (arz, apc/ajp, afb, ary), and different editions disagree. Verify against current Ethnologue before quoting.
- Middlebury calls Levantine "the next most commonly spoken dialect after Egyptian" — [Middlebury Language Schools](https://www.middlebury.edu/language-schools/blog/which-arabic-dialect-to-learn)

**Empirical intelligibility (Trentman & Shiri 2020)**
- Design: an online listening test with passages in North African, Egyptian, Levantine and Gulf Arabic and in MSA. 55 native speakers (NS) and 62 non-native learners (NNS) were analysed.
  - NS comprehension: familiar dialect 96%, MSA 95%, unfamiliar dialects 89%.
  - NNS comprehension: MSA 96%, their familiar dialect 91%, unfamiliar dialects 83%.
  - For learners, the significant predictors of understanding an unfamiliar dialect were (a) being able to identify their own familiar dialect and (b) being able to identify MSA. In other words, a solid single dialect plus MSA awareness transfers to other dialects.
  - The authors note that Gulf Arabic was the most familiar dialect for relatively few learners, reflecting pedagogy trends.
  - Source: [Trentman & Shiri 2020, "The Mutual Intelligibility of Arabic Dialects"](https://journals.librarypublishing.arizona.edu/cms/article/6549/galley/6052/download/)

**Pronunciation markers by dialect**
- Qaf is realised as [g] in Bedouin dialects and in urban Arabian Peninsula/Gulf varieties, and as a glottal stop [ʔ] in urban Egypt and the urban Levant. Other reflexes include [q], [k], [dʒ] and [ɣ] — [Wikipedia: Varieties of Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varieties_of_Arabic)
- In Egyptian, jim is pronounced [g] (najm becomes negm). In Palestinian, qaf can be [g] or [k], and k can become ch, depending on the area. Gulf varieties often use [g] for qaf and sometimes ch for k — [Middle East Eye](https://www.middleeasteye.net/discover/five-major-spoken-arabic-dialects-unique)

  - Maghrebi permanent residents, end of 2010: Morocco 7,469; Tunisia 6,418; Algeria 5,822. These come from a University of Neuchâtel study commissioned by the State Secretariat for Migration (SEM).
  - 67% of Moroccans, 64% of Algerians and 56% of Tunisians live in French-speaking Romandy. More than half of Moroccans and Algerians live in Geneva and Vaud.
  - Lebanese: about 16,000 (Joshua Project, undated, low reliability).
  - Iraqis: about 7,185 (2013).
- Syrian refugees in Switzerland: 20,146 — [Arab Center DC](https://arabcenterdc.org/resource/syrian-refugee-population-around-the-globe/)

### Inferences
- **Recommendation: Levantine (urban Shami) as the primary dialect.** Reasons:
  - (a) It is the second most widely understood dialect after Egyptian, helped by Syrian/Lebanese drama and Gulf-funded pan-Arab TV and dubbing.
  - (b) It is phonologically and lexically closer to MSA than Egyptian or Darija, which makes the MSA bridge cheaper.
  - (c) It has the largest recent diaspora in German-speaking Europe (Syrians) and a sizeable Lebanese presence in Geneva.
  - (d) It has abundant learner resources (Lingualism, LevantineArabic.com, Lingua Verna, Talk In Arabic, Pimsleur Eastern Arabic, Mango).
  - Pick one anchor accent (for example Damascus/Beirut "madani" with ʔ for qaf). Mark regional variants in footnotes (Palestinian/Jordanian g/k for qaf, Lebanese "shu/kif", Syrian "shu/kīf"). Do not blend them in the main dialogue.
- **Strongest alternative: Egyptian.** It wins on raw speakers and media (film, comedy, music, YouTube), is understood almost everywhere, and is simple to pronounce (g for jim). It costs a bit more distance from MSA, and Egypt has a smaller Swiss and European diaspora. A product could offer a parallel "Egyptian track" reusing the same scenario skeleton.
- **Gulf:** It carries prestige and jobs (UAE/Saudi expat work). But the Gulf has few native speakers relative to its foreign residents, and English or "foreigner pidgin" dominates expat life there. Learner resources are thinner, and Trentman & Shiri note that few learners have it as their familiar dialect. Good only for users relocating to the Gulf.
- **Moroccan Darija:** The most locally relevant community in Romandy (Geneva/Vaud) and France. However, it is the least intelligible to Mashriqis, heavily mixes in French and Amazigh, and has the weakest transfer to other dialects. Best as a separate optional course, not the default. Maghrebis generally understand Levantine and Egyptian, so a Levantine speaker can still communicate with them.
- **MSA bridge, without breaking the speaking focus:** Every 5th to 7th lesson, add a short "Fusha corner". It would cover reading a sign or menu, a headline, or a WhatsApp message from an official, or listening to 20 seconds of news, all contrasted with the dialect equivalents (for example dialect "shu" vs MSA "mādhā"; dialect b-present vs MSA bare imperfect; "mish/ma…sh" vs "lā/lam/lan/laysa"). Scale it up in the last 30 lessons: news, a formal speech, a religious register, and the "white dialect" / educated spoken Arabic used on pan-Arab talk shows.
- Trentman & Shiri suggest learners who can identify both their dialect and MSA understand unfamiliar dialects best. This supports including a cross-dialect listening lesson around the B2 level (the same short dialogue in Egyptian, Gulf and Darija).

### Gaps
- There is no current FSO count of Arabic main-language speakers or Arab nationalities in Switzerland for 2023–2025; the FSO tables were not retrievable in this session. Swiss numbers above are dated (2010–2015) or secondary.
- Reliable current speaker counts per dialect: sources conflict, and Ethnologue was not accessed directly.
- I could not confirm whether Assimil ever published a full "sans peine" Egyptian or Lebanese course. Only phrasebooks were found.

## 2. Grammar sequencing for Levantine (with Egyptian parallels)

### Takeaway
Spoken dialects drop case endings and most of MSA's verbal mood morphology. Speaking progress therefore depends on pronoun suffixes, the b- present / future particle system, negation, and a high-frequency verb inventory, not on case grammar. Root-and-pattern morphology (forms I–X, broken plurals) should be introduced *inductively*, as vocabulary-building insight, from about the B1 level.

### Cited Findings
- Lingualism's free Levantine materials centre on full conjugation tables of common verbs with example sentences, which supports a verb-paradigm-heavy progression — [Lingualism Levantine posts](https://lingualism.com/posts/posts-category-egyptian-arabic-or-levantine-arabic); [Lingualism verbs series](https://lingualism.com/posts/posts-category-egyptian-arabic-or-levantine-arabic/posts-series-grammar-or-verbs)

### Inferences (dialectology knowledge, not web-verified this session)
Proposed sequence. Levantine is given first, Egyptian after "/":
1. **L1–10: Pronouns and copula-less sentences**
   - Pronouns: ana, inta/inti, huwwe/hiyye, niḥna (Eg. iḥna), intu, hinne (Eg. humma).
   - No present-tense "to be": "ana mabsūṭ".
   - Demonstratives: hāda/hāy/hadōl (Eg. da/di/dōl).
   - Question words: shu/ēsh (Eg. ēh, placed after the verb), mīn, wēn (Eg. fēn), ēmta (Eg. imta), kīf (Eg. izzāy), lēsh (Eg. lēh), addēsh (Eg. bikām/ʔaddi ēh).
2. **L5–15: Possessive suffixes** (-i, -ak/-ik, -o/-ha, -na, -kon, -hon) on nouns and prepositions. This includes ʕand- ("I have" = ʕandi) and maʕ-.
   - Pseudo-verbs bidd- (Lev. "want": biddi) / Eg. ʕāyez (participle); fī ("there is").
   - Negation of these with ma/mā fī, ma ʕandi, ma biddi (Eg. ma ʕandīsh, mafīsh, mish ʕāyez).
3. **L10–25: Imperfect + b- prefix for present/habitual**: biḥki, btiḥki, byiḥki. The bare imperfect is used after modals ("biddi rūḥ", "lāzem iḥki", "fīni sāʕdak").
   - Future: raḥ / ḥa- (Lev. raḥ rūḥ; Eg. ḥa-rūḥ).
   - Progressive: ʕam (Lev. ʕam bikteb; Eg. uses bi- or the participle).
   - Negation of verbs: ma + verb (Lev.); ma…sh circumfix (Eg. ma-katabtish; also in Palestinian/Southern Lev.); mish/mu for non-verbal predicates and future (Lev. mish raḥ; Syr. mu).
4. **L20–35: Perfect tense** (katab, katabt, katabna…), concentrating on the hollow verbs (rāḥ/ruḥt, ʔāl/ʔult, kān/kunt), defective verbs (ḥaka/ḥakēt, nisi/nsīt) and doubled verbs (ḥabb/ḥabbēt) that dominate speech.
   - kān + imperfect for past habitual; kān + participle.
5. **L25–40: Imperative**: rūḥ/rūḥi/rūḥu, taʕa/taʕi (Lev. "come"), ḥuṭṭ, khalli.
   - Negative imperative: ma truḥ / (Eg. ma-truḥsh).
   - Common irregulars: jāb/yjīb, ʔija/yiji (Eg. geh/yīgi), akal/yākol, akhad/yākhod.
6. **Gender agreement and the feminine -e/-a ending**; adjective agreement. Non-human plurals usually take feminine singular or plural agreement, depending on the speaker.
7. **Numbers** (lessons spread from 10 to 50):
   - 1 and 2 follow the noun ("kāse wiḥde"); the **dual** -ēn is productive in dialect for countable nouns ("kāstēn").
   - 3–10 take a plural noun, using the short "-t" linking forms (tlat t-iyyām).
   - 11+ take a singular noun ("ʕishrīn dʔīʔa").
   - Money, time and prices need early drills.
   - Teach the colloquial forms (tlēte, arbʕa, khamse, sitte, sabʕa, tmēne, tisʕa, ʕashara) and Lira/Franc/Dollar contexts.
8. **Idafa (construct state)** from about L15: "bēt ahli", "fenjān ahwe". Teach the tāʔ marbūṭa → -it change (sayyārit abūy).
   - Also the analytic possessive tabaʕ / taba (Lev.) and bitāʕ (Eg.): "is-sayyāra tabaʕi".
9. **Relative "illi"** (from about L30). Relative clauses on indefinite nouns take no illi.
10. **Participles** (from about L30): rāyiḥ, jāy (Eg. gayy), ʕāref, fāhem, sāken, nāyem, mitʔakked, mistanni. These carry present-perfect and stative meanings ("ana wāṣel" = I've arrived / am arriving).
11. **Modals**:
    - lāzem (must); mumkin (possible/may); fī / fīni (Lev. "I can"; Eg. aʔdar).
    - ʕam biddo (Lev. "it's about to"); ṣār (became / already, "ṣār-li sāʕa", "I've been here an hour").
    - ʕala bāli (I feel like); khalli + subjunctive (let).
12. **Conditionals** (B1): iza / law (Lev. iza = real, law = hypothetical); Eg. law for both, iza rarer; in + jussive is formulaic ("in shāʔ allāh").
    - Patterns: "law kunt baʕref, kint ʔeltillak"; "iza bitrīd" (please, if you like).
13. **Root-and-pattern / verb forms I–X** (B1–B2), taught inductively from already-known verbs:
    - II: ʕallam (to teach), causative/intensive.
    - III: sāʕad (to help), reciprocal-directed.
    - V: tʕallam (to learn), reflexive of II.
    - VI: tʕāwanu (to cooperate).
    - VII: inbasaṭ (to have fun / be pleased), passive-like.
    - VIII: ishtaghal (to work), iḥtaram (to respect).
    - X: istaʕmal (to use), istanna (to wait).
    - Pair this with noun patterns maf'ūl, maf'al(a), fāʕil, mufaʕʕil, so learners can guess unknown words.
14. **Broken plurals** (from about L15, as lexical items; patterns explained at B1): walad/wlād, bēt/byūt, kitāb/kutub, ṣāḥeb/ṣḥāb.
    - Sound plurals -īn/-āt.
    - Collective nouns with -e singulative: tuffāḥ → tuffāḥa.
15. **Object suffixes and double objects** (B1): ʔultillo (I told him). Egyptian negative + object stacking: ma-ʔultilhumsh.
16. **Discourse and stance markers** (B1–C1): yaʕni, ya ʕammi, khalaṣ, ṭayyib, ballah (Lev.), hēk, ʕa fikra (by the way), walla (really/by God), mbala (Lev. "yes, contrary to negative question"), wēnak ya zalame.
17. **C1–C2**: reported speech, register shifting toward MSA in formal speech ("white dialect"), proverbs, and rhetorical oaths.
- Script and transliteration sequencing (see section 5): use transliteration only in L1–15 while introducing the script gradually.

### Gaps
- I found no published frequency-ranked grammar syllabus for Levantine or Egyptian to cite. The sequence above is a design proposal.

## 3. High-frequency vocabulary sources and core social formulae

### Takeaway
The only formal frequency dictionary (Buckwalter & Parkinson) is MSA-centric but marks dialect distribution. For dialect frequency, the practical sources are parallel dialect corpora (MADAR), subtitle and social-media corpora, and curated learner lists (Lingualism, Living Arabic). Social formulae must be taught as a core, recurring layer from lesson 1.

### Cited Findings
- *A Frequency Dictionary of Arabic: Core Vocabulary for Learners* (Buckwalter & Parkinson, Routledge 2011) is built on a **30-million-word corpus** of written and spoken material from the entire Arab world. It covers the **5,000 most frequent words in MSA**, each with a sample sentence, usage stats, genre variation and usage distribution across several major dialects. It is indexed by root and includes thematic lists (family, weather, clothing, sports). A processable text file is supplied as online support material — [Routledge](https://www.routledge.com/A-Frequency-Dictionary-of-Arabic-Core-Vocabulary-for-Learners/Buckwalter-Parkinson/p/book/9780415444347)
- **MADAR corpus** (Bouamor et al., LREC 2018) is a parallel travel-domain corpus covering the dialects of **25 Arab cities** plus English, French and MSA.
  - 2,000 sentences (from the Basic Traveling Expression Corpus, BTEC) were translated into all 25 city dialects; a further 10,000 sentences were translated into 5 cities' dialects.
  - It comes with a **lexicon of 1,045 concepts × about 45 words per concept across the 25 cities**.
  - The licence covers internal research and evaluation only (English/French source text via the U-Star Consortium). It is ideal for pulling city-specific translations of scenario sentences (Cairo, Damascus, Beirut, Amman, Jerusalem, Rabat, Doha, and others).
  - Sources: [MADAR LREC 2018 paper](https://camel.abudhabi.nyu.edu/madar/static/pdfs/2018-LREC-MADAR-Resources.pdf); [CAMeL Lab download](https://camel.abudhabi.nyu.edu/madar-parallel-corpus)
- Lingualism provides free Levantine and Egyptian vocabulary and verb resources, and premium audio vocabulary courses — [Lingualism](https://lingualism.com/posts/posts-category-egyptian-arabic-or-levantine-arabic)
- Nile-Chat (2025) built Egyptian LLMs for both Arabic script and Latin-script Arabizi. This shows that large-scale Egyptian corpora in both scripts now exist — [arXiv 2507.04569](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.04569v1)

### Inferences (not web-verified this session)
- Vocabulary pipeline:
  - (1) Take Buckwalter & Parkinson's top about 2,000 lemmas and keep those marked as common in dialects.
  - (2) Swap in dialect equivalents for MSA-only function words. For example: mādhā → shu/ēh; ayna → wēn/fēn; al-ān → halla/dilwaʔti; arāda → bidd-/ʕāyez; ra'ā → shāf; dhahaba → rāḥ; jāʔa → ʔija/geh; kathīr → ktīr/ʔawi; jayyid → mnīḥ/kwayyes; ṣaghīr → zghīr/ṣughayyar.
  - (3) Validate with MADAR's lexicon and OpenSubtitles Lebanese/Syrian/Egyptian dramas.
  - (4) Cross-check against Living Arabic (livingarabic.com dictionaries) and Lisaan Masry for Egyptian.
- Core first-50 spoken words: ah/la, ē/aywa, mashi/māshi, ṭayyib, khalaṣ, yaʕni, halla/hallaʔ, hōn/hnāk (Eg. hena/hināk), ktīr, shwayy, kamān, bass, lissa, baʕdēn, abl, mbāreḥ, bukra, il-yōm, hēk (Eg. kida), kull, shī, ḥada, mish, fī, ʕand-, bidd-, lāzem, mumkin, baʕref/ma baʕref, fhemt, shukran, ʕafwan, mnīḥ, tamām, akīd, yalla, ḥabībi/ḥabībti, inta/inti, shu, wēn, ēmta, kīf, addēsh, lēsh, mīn, ʔaddēsh ḥaʔʔo.
- **Social and religious formulae** (teach as call-and-response pairs, since that is how they are used):
  - Greetings:
    - marḥaba → marḥabtēn / ahlēn
    - is-salāmu ʕalaykum → wa ʕalaykum is-salām
    - ṣabāḥ il-khēr → ṣabāḥ in-nūr / ṣabāḥ il-ward
    - masa l-khēr → masa n-nūr
  - Welcome: ahlan w sahlan → ahlan fīk/fīki.
  - "How are you" marathon: kīfak? → il-ḥamdilla / mnīḥ, w inta?
  - Thanks:
    - tislam / tislamī (thanks) → allāh yisallmak
    - yaʕṭīk il-ʕāfye (to someone working, or as a greeting) → allāh yʕāfīk
  - Willingness: ʕala rāsi / ʕala ʕēni ("with pleasure, my pleasure"); taḥt amrak.
  - God-related phrases:
    - inshallah (hope / maybe / polite no)
    - mashallah (admiration; wards off the evil eye)
    - allāh yikhallīk (please)
    - allāh yirḥamo (of the dead)
  - Meals: ṣaḥtēn / bil-hana w ish-shifa (bon appétit) → ʕala albak.
  - Recovery and relief: ḥamdilla ʕas-salāme (after a trip or illness) → allāh yisallmak; salāmtak (get well) → allāh yisallmak.
  - Condolence: ʕaẓẓam allāh ajrak / il-baʔiyye b-ḥayātak → ḥayātak il-baʔiyye.
  - Congratulations: mabrūk / alf mabrūk → allāh ybārek fīk.
  - Holidays:
    - Ramadan: Ramaḍān karīm → allāhu akram; Ramaḍān mubārak.
    - Eid: ʕīd mubārak / kull ʕām w intu b-khēr → w inta b-ṣaḥḥa w salāme.
    - After a shower or haircut: naʕīman.
  - Leaving: maʕ is-salāme / bkhāṭrak → maʕ is-salāme / allāh maʕak.
  - Apology: walla ma ʔaṣdi; muʔākhze (excuse me, Lev.).
  - Lev. "sorry / excuse me": ʕafwan / sorry / pardon.

## 4. Modern 2026 slang, Arabizi and code-switching

### Takeaway
Arabizi (Latin script with numerals 2/3/5/7/9) remains common in informal chat, especially among youth and in diaspora settings. Code-switching is pervasive: English in the Levant, Egypt and the Gulf; French in Lebanon and the Maghreb. Youth slang is fast-moving and highly local, so a course should teach a stable, attested core and refresh a small slang appendix yearly.

### Cited Findings
- **Arabizi numeral mapping:**

  | Numeral | Letter |
  |---|---|
  | 2 | ء hamza (and Egyptian glottal qaf) |
  | 3 | ع ʿayn |
  | 3' / 8 | غ ghayn (8 for ghayn only in Lebanon) |
  | 5 / 7' | خ kha |
  | 6 | ط ṭa |
  | 6' | ظ ẓa |

  | 9 | ص ṣad |
  | 9' | ض ḍad |
  | 8 / 9 | ق qaf (mainly in Saudi Arabia) |

  — [Wikipedia: Arabic chat alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_chat_alphabet)
- **Regional Arabizi conventions:**
  - Egypt: g for jīm.
  - Levant: j for jīm; Lebanese uses capital D/T for emphatics; $ for shīn in Jordan.
  - Maghreb: French-style spelling (ch for ش, dj especially in Algeria, é/è).
  - Source: [Wikipedia: Arabic chat alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_chat_alphabet)
- Arabizi arose with SMS, IRC and the early web, which lacked Arabic support. It is mostly used by youth informally, is never used formally, and is rarely used for long texts. Many users keep using it even when Arabic keyboards are available, because they are familiar with QWERTY or AZERTY layouts. Google and Microsoft translators can convert it. Educators and pan-Arabists criticise it. A 2020 Nazareth study found a high degree of spelling normativisation — [Wikipedia: Arabic chat alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_chat_alphabet)
- Arabizi use across three generations has been studied academically — [Haghegh, AWEJ](https://awej-tls.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/12-1.pdf)
- Egyptian LLM work (Nile-Chat, 2025) explicitly targets both Arabic and Latin scripts, which indicates Arabizi remains a live input mode in Egypt — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.04569v1)
- Gen Z/Alpha slang in Cairo is fast enough that a parent-facing Instagram/TikTok account translates it for parents. Ahram Online (Dec 2025) reports the account creator researches terms through interviews and TikTok trends — [Ahram Online](https://english.ahram.org.eg/News/558362.aspx)
- Examples of youth slang from a commercial learning blog (low reliability; verify before use):
  - Egyptian "damār" (lit. "destruction") = amazing.
  - "ḥarīʔa" (fire) = amazing.
  - English loans "kyūt" and "bēst".
  - Gen Z slang varies strongly by region.
  - Source: [Talkpal](https://talkpal.ai/vocabulary/top-10-arabic-gen-z-slang-terms-you-need-to-know/)
- Egyptian millennials actively reshape Arabic with English mixing — [The National 2022](https://www.thenationalnews.com/mena/egypt/2022/04/15/how-egypts-millennials-are-making-arabic-their-own)

### Inferences (not web-verified this session)
- Teach code-switching explicitly as a native feature, not an error.
  - Lebanese "Hi, kīfak, ça va?"; "merci"; "bonjour".
  - English verbs with Arabic morphology: "baʕmel save", "ʕmelt-lo block", "msayyev", "tdallaʕ".
  - Common loans: "cancel", "meeting", "deadline", "weekend", "bro", "ok khalaṣ", "seriously?", "literally".
- Relatively stable informal Levantine items: yalla, khalaṣ, ʕanjad (seriously?), walla?, shu ṣāyer?, ktīr ḥelo, tamām, ʕal-ʔalīl (at least), ya zalame (dude), ʕīb (shame on you / don't mention it), maʕlēsh, ballash (Lev. "stop it"), ʕa rāḥtak (take your time), mashi l-ḥāl.
  - Egyptian equivalents: ya ʕamm, ya basha (respectful, older), gāmed (cool), ʔaṭṭaʕ (to cut off / ghost), fakes (forget it, from English "fake"; unverified), ēh il-akhbār, ya sīdi, khalāṣ.
  - All of these should be validated with native consultants in 2026.
- Course design: keep slang in a dated "Street Arabic 2026" sidebar per lesson, flagged as revisable, and keep the main dialogues in neutral-informal register.

### Gaps
- No authoritative 2025–2026 slang list for Levantine or Gulf youth was found. The Egyptian slang data comes from low-reliability commercial blogs. This needs native-speaker review.
- No usage statistics were found comparing Arabizi with Arabic script in 2025–2026 messaging.

## 5. Script, transliteration and pronunciation

### Takeaway
Use a **dual presentation**:
- a learner-friendly scholarly-lite transliteration (ʔ, ʕ, ḥ, kh, gh, dots for emphatics, long vowels doubled or with macrons);
- vowelled Arabic script in dialect spelling;
- Arabizi taught as a reading skill (for chats), not as the main teaching medium.

The hardest sounds (ʕ, ḥ, emphatics, q/ʔ) need dedicated lessons in the first 10–15 units.

### Cited Findings
- Qaf realisation varies by dialect: urban Levant and Egypt [ʔ]; Gulf and Bedouin [g]; plus [q], [k], [dʒ] — [Wikipedia: Varieties of Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Varieties_of_Arabic)
- Egyptian [g] for jīm; Palestinian/Gulf ch for k in some areas — [Middle East Eye](https://www.middleeasteye.net/discover/five-major-spoken-arabic-dialects-unique)
- Arabizi numerals cover precisely the sounds absent from English/French (ʕ, ḥ, kh, ʔ, emphatics), which makes Arabizi a convenient bridge for learners as well — [Wikipedia: Arabic chat alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_chat_alphabet)
- Lingualism publishes material on Levantine orthography, meaning how to spell dialect in Arabic script (there is no official standard) — [Lingualism Levantine orthography](https://lingualism.com/posts/posts-category-egyptian-arabic-or-levantine-arabic/posts-tags-grammar-or-orthography-or-vocabulary/)

### Inferences (not web-verified this session)
- Dialect Arabic-script spelling is non-standard. Follow the conventions Lingualism and LevantineArabic.com use: keep the etymological ق and ث even when pronounced ʔ/t/s, so learners can read MSA later; write b- as بـ; write raḥ as رح.
- Pronunciation module order:
  1. Long vs short vowels; the stress system.
  2. ḥ (ح) vs h; kh (خ); gh (غ).
  3. ʕ (ع) and ʔ (ء/ق); mnemonic minimal pairs: ʕēn/ʔēn, ḥāl/hāl, sēf/ṣēf, tīn/ṭīn.
  4. Emphatics ṣ ḍ ṭ ẓ and their effect on neighbouring vowels ("dark" a).
  5. Gemination (shadda): ḥamām (pigeon) vs ḥammām (bathroom).
  6. Levantine imāla (final -a → -e: "madrase").
  7. Dialect markers per region (Syrian/Lebanese vs Palestinian/Jordanian).
- Gradual script integration:
  - L1–10: transliteration plus audio only.
  - L11–30: script alongside transliteration.
  - L31+: script-first, with transliteration on tap (the app can toggle).
  - Include an "Arabizi reading" mini-exercise every ~10 lessons (WhatsApp screenshots).

### Gaps
- No empirical study on whether learners do better with script-first or translit-first in dialect courses was located in this session.

## 6. Scenario topics graded A1 → C2 (for about 100–120 lessons)

### Takeaway
Arabic social life is formula-heavy: greetings, hospitality and condolence rituals are high-frequency, *obligatory* speech acts. They belong early and should recur. Later levels move to narration, humour, sarcasm, sensitive topics and register-shifting toward MSA.

### Cited Findings
- Assimil's Lebanese phrasebook organises content into 45 thematic chapters, a useful benchmark for scenario coverage — [Assimil Arabe libanais de poche](https://assimil.com/en/phrasebooks/168-arabe-libanais-de-poche-9782700503579.html)
- The MADAR corpus's travel/daily-life (BTEC) sentences provide ready parallel material across 25 cities for transactional scenarios (taxi, hotel, restaurant, shopping, directions) — [MADAR LREC 2018](https://camel.abudhabi.nyu.edu/madar/static/pdfs/2018-LREC-MADAR-Resources.pdf)

### Inferences (proposed list; not web-verified)
**A1 (L1–20)**
1. Greetings marathon (marḥaba/ahlan, salām; the repeated "kīfak? ḥamdilla" loops).
2. Introducing yourself (name, country: "ana min Swisra", languages).
3. Coffee and tea hospitality (accepting/refusing; "ṣaḥtēn", the third-offer custom; shaking the cup to signal "enough" for Bedouin/Gulf qahwa).
4. Numbers, prices, the bakery / furn (manʔūshe).
5. Taxi and directions ("ʕal-yamīn, dughri, hōn bass").
6. Family questions ("mitjawwez? ʕandak wlād? allāh yikhallīlak yāhon").
7. Food and the restaurant (hummus, falafel, shawarma; ordering; "il-ḥsāb law samaḥt").
8. Time and days; making plans ("bukra inshallah").
9. Phone and WhatsApp basics; Arabizi reading.
10. Shopping and the market; basic bargaining.

**A2 (L21–40)**
- The souq: bargaining script ("ākher siʕr?", "ghāli ktīr", "ʕala ḥsābak / ʕalayy").
- Neighbours and the building's caretaker (nāṭūr/bawwāb).
- Hosting and visiting: the invitation dance ("lā, walla ma bṣīr!"), fighting over the bill.
- Weather and seasons; the weekend picnic (mashāwi).
- Health basics: pharmacy, "salāmtak".
- Transport: service taxi, bus, microbus.
- Hobbies; football small talk (Barça/Real, local clubs, national teams).
- Ramadan basics: iftar invitation, "Ramaḍān karīm", suhoor, fasting etiquette for non-Muslims.
- Compliments and evil-eye etiquette: say "mashallah" when praising children, homes or cars; "ʕēn il-ḥasūd"; "allāh yiḥmī".

**B1 (L41–65)**
- The doctor's visit (symptoms, past tense narration).
- The landlord: renting a flat, a broken boiler, the deposit.
- Job interview / workplace small talk; the boss; office culture ("baʕd shwayy" time).
- Eid al-Fitr / al-Adha visits, ʕīdiyye, maʕmūl sweets.
- Weddings: engagement (khuṭbe), zaffe, congratulations, "ʕuʔbālak" (may you be next).
- Telling a story in the past: "kunt māshi w faj'atan…".
- Complaints and polite refusals ("inshallah", "mnshūf").
- Bureaucracy: residency papers, the bank, the embassy.
- Social media, phones, delivery apps.
- Cooking with a mother-in-law figure (recipes, imperative chains).

**B2 (L66–85)**
- Condolences and the ʕaza (funeral reception): fixed formulae, Qur'an recitation context, bitter coffee.
- Arguing about football; teasing.
- Expressing opinions on city life, prices and the economy (the Lebanese crisis, rebuilding in Syria): handled neutrally.
- Gender and family expectations: marriage questions to singles ("ēmta nfraḥ fīk?").
- Hospital stay; car accident; insurance.
- Hypotheticals and regrets ("law kint…").
- Cross-dialect listening day (the same scene in Egyptian, Gulf and Darija).
- Fusha corner: reading the news headline, then discussing it in dialect.

**C1 (L86–105)**
- Jokes, irony and sarcasm: Egyptian nukta culture; Syrian/Lebanese self-deprecation; reading tone.
- Politics and religion sensitivity: how natives deflect ("khallīna min is-siyāse"), safe vs unsafe topics, sectarian sensitivities in Lebanon.
- Proverbs (amthāl) in context.
- Storytelling (ḥakawāti style); childhood memories.
- Negotiating at work; giving a toast or a speech at a wedding.
- Media: a talk show in "white dialect"; a song analysis (Fairuz, Umm Kulthum, current pop/rap).

**C2 (L106–120)**
- Register-switching: from street to formal MSA (presenting at a conference, a formal letter read aloud).
- Debating a social issue; mediating a family dispute.
- Humour improv; wordplay; regional imitation (identifying accents).
- Religious registers: Friday-sermon vocabulary, Qur'anic formula recognition.
- Free conversation capstones.

### Gaps
- No published CEFR-aligned dialect scenario syllabus was found to benchmark against. The list is a design proposal.

## 7. Existing courses and apps (competitive landscape)

### Takeaway
The market has many dialect resources, but few long, structured, audio-dialogue-driven courses that go beyond A2 in dialect. That is the gap an Assimil-style 100+ lesson Levantine course would fill.

### Cited Findings
- **Assimil**: MSA "L'arabe" with 77 lessons (A1–A2) plus a B1–B2 "Perfectionnement" volume; dialects only as phrasebooks (Lebanese, Moroccan, Tunisian, Algerian; an Egyptian kit) — [Assimil](https://www.assimil.com/fr/9755-apprendre-larabe)
- **Pimsleur**: Modern Standard Arabic Levels 1–3. The Egyptian course is listed at Level 1 (older "Arabic (Egy) Phase 1" units), and Pimsleur also mentions an "Eastern Arabic" course (likely Levantine; unconfirmed) — [Pimsleur MSA](https://pimsleur.com/learn-arabic-modern-standard); [Audible Egyptian Phase 1](https://origin-www.audible.com/pd/Arabic-Egy-Phase-1-Unit-01-05-Audiobook/B004GHY4NW); [All Language Resources review](https://www.alllanguageresources.com/pimsleur-arabic-review/)
- **Mango Languages**: conversation-based Levantine, Egyptian and Iraqi courses — [Mango](https://mangolanguages.com/?p=369)
- **Lingualism** (Matthew Aldrich): free Levantine/Egyptian verb conjugation, pronunciation and orthography posts, plus premium audio vocabulary series — [Lingualism](https://lingualism.com/posts/posts-category-egyptian-arabic-or-levantine-arabic)
- **Lingua Verna** (Levantine-focused): mostly positive 2025–2026 Trustpilot reviews praising structure and conversational focus (self-selected reviews) — [Trustpilot](https://uk.trustpilot.com/review/linguaverna.com)
- University of Oregon CASLS lists online Levantine/Egyptian resources — [CASLS InterCom](https://caslsintercom.uoregon.edu/content/28638)
- Livingarabic.com, Talk In Arabic, LevantineArabic.com, Kalaam and Speak Arabic Like a Native were named in the brief but **not verified** in this session.

### Inferences
- Differentiators for the new course:
  - (1) 100+ lessons reaching B2/C1 in dialect, which most products stop short of.
  - (2) Assimil-style humorous recurring characters (for example a Swiss expat in Beirut/Amman with a Syrian family in Geneva) for cultural realism and Swiss relevance.
  - (3) An integrated MSA bridge.
  - (4) A yearly refreshed slang/Arabizi layer.
  - (5) Optional Egyptian and Darija "mirror" tracks built on the same scenario skeleton.

### Gaps
- Current catalogues and prices for Talk In Arabic, LevantineArabic.com, Kalaam, Arabic Unlocked and Speak Arabic Like a Native could not be verified in this session.
- Pimsleur's full Egyptian/Eastern Arabic level structure is unconfirmed.
